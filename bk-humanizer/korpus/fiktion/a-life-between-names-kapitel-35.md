# A Life Between Names — Kapitel 35, Anfang

Kathy called on the Tuesday, no preamble, the way she always did — the first Tuesday of the month meant Velvet, and had done for months now, ever since Kathy and Stef had quietly decided, somewhere in that first disorienting week after the transformation, that they weren’t going to let me disappear into my own flat and stay there. Neither of them had ever put it in so many words. They’d simply started calling, every month, without fail, and never once let me say no.

The dress code wasn’t really a rule so much as a fact about the address: Velvet was the place in the city you went if you wanted to see and be seen, and there wasn’t much point turning up in anything less than your best — skin on show, heels that meant business. Nobody required that of me specifically. It simply came with the venue, the way a dress code comes with any place that takes itself seriously. Which suited me better than I usually admitted; I’d never once needed convincing on that particular front. I liked getting dressed up too much to call it a chore.

“You heard, then,” I said, because of course she had. Word always found Kathy.

“That you’re a free woman again? Obviously. Which is exactly why tonight matters more than usual. You know the drill — dress to kill, heels as usual. Eight o’clock. Stef’s already got the table.”

I didn’t need convincing, and I wasn’t going to pretend otherwise. I hadn’t broken things off with Mike because I wanted less of this — the club, the heat, the being-looked-at. I’d broken it off because somewhere in the last few weeks it had started asking for more of me than I’d meant to give, and tonight was the exact opposite of that: no one expecting anything, no one keeping score, just two friends who’d known the truth of all this from the beginning, ever since Anja first sat them down and explained what was actually going on, and had never once made it feel strange since. I’d asked for this — all of it, the body and the looking and the wanting to be looked at — and I wasn’t about to sit home on the one night a month that belonged to the three of us and no one else.

We got ready at Stef’s, the way we always did — a bottle of prosecco open on the counter before anyone had done a single eyelash, three phones arguing over the music, the particular chaos of three women fighting for the same stretch of mirror. I did my own face, start to finish, the way I had for months now: foundation, the cat-eye I’d finally got the knack of after a dozen ruined attempts, the lip colour that did most of the actual work. Kathy watched me blend concealer under my eyes over the rim of her glass and shook her head slowly, more impressed than she’d probably admit to.

“It still gets me,” she said, “that you spent twenty-six years not knowing what a beauty blender was, and now you’re better at this than either of us.”

“Good genes,” I said.

“Good genes had nothing to do with it,” Stef called from the mirror, wrestling a mascara wand into submission. “She practiced. Like an absolute lunatic. For months.”

I let them have that one — it was true — and said nothing else, just watched my own reflection carry on doing what it did now without my having to think about it, which was, itself, still the best part, months in.

Velvet at ten was heat and bass and a version of me that didn’t have to try. I’d spent the whole of Alex’s life working out what to say to make a woman look twice; here, I only had to stand near the bar in the right dress and let it happen to me, which felt, for a good four songs, like the quiet correction of some long-standing unfairness.

“You’re doing that thing again,” Stef shouted over the music, delighted. “Where you don’t notice you’re doing it.”

“Doing what?”

“Existing at people.”

Around midnight the three of us crowded into a cubicle meant for one, the way we always did, to fix lipstick and compare notes. Stef had spent forty minutes on a bartender’s forearms and had, by the sound of it, already secured his number and the exact minute his shift ended. Kathy had turned down two men so far with visible satisfaction and was, by her own account, saving herself for a third.

“And you,” Kathy said, reapplying gloss without looking away from the mirror, “have had the one in the grey shirt watching you for twenty minutes. Are you going to do something about that, or just enjoy it?”

“I’m doing something about it,” I said, and watched both their faces do something delighted and faintly disbelieving at once. Kathy actually set her lip gloss down.

“Look at you,” she said. “Eye contact and everything. At the beginning you couldn’t hold a conversation with a barista.”

“That was Alex,” I said. “I’ve had practice since.”

He was easy to convince into buying me a drink once I gave the right signals — good jaw, easy laugh, the specific unbothered confidence of a man who’d never once had to wonder if a woman was interested. I didn’t ask his name twice, and I’m not going to give it here; it isn’t the part that matters. We danced badly at first, half a beat behind the music and overcorrecting for it, until somewhere in the third song neither of us was counting anymore and there was no distance left between his chest and mine that hadn’t been closed on purpose. My arms found their way around his neck without my quite deciding to put them there, and his hands settled at my waist, then slid — slow, unhurried, testing whether I’d stop him — down over the curve of my hip, and stayed there when I didn’t. His jaw grazed the top of my head, which rested at his shoulder. Cedar, I registered, under the noise and the heat of the room — cedar, and something warmer beneath it that was just him.

“I like the smell of your hair,” he murmured, close enough that I felt the words more than heard them.

I laughed, once, into his collar. It hadn’t occurred to me, until just then, that this went both ways.

Stef left first, a little after one, towing the bartender by the hand the moment his shift ended, laughing at something he’d said before she’d even got her coat on. Kathy went not long after with her third man, the one she’d apparently been holding out for all along, catching my eye on the way past the door and mouthing something that needed no translation at all. Which left me and the grey shirt, and Velvet doing precisely what Velvet was built to do. He didn’t say anything. He just took my hand — his palm swallowing mine whole, my head tipping back to find his face at an angle I barely registered anymore — and steered us both toward the taxi stand, easy about it, as if the decision had already been made somewhere neither of us had needed to say out loud.
